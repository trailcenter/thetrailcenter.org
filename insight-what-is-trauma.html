What Is Trauma?

Trauma is not only an event. It is the lasting impact an overwhelming experience can have on the body, mind, relationships, behavior, and life course.

Trauma can begin with experiences that overwhelm a person’s capacity to cope, threaten safety or connection, or leave too little power, support, or possibility for escape. The event matters, but trauma also lives in what happens afterward: how the nervous system adapts, how meaning is made, how relationships change, and whether environments support recovery or deepen harm.

Judith Herman’s work emphasizes that psychological trauma can disrupt a person’s sense of safety, trust, autonomy, connection, and meaning. Recovery therefore requires more than recalling an event; it involves rebuilding safety, agency, relationships, and a workable future.

Bessel van der Kolk explains how traumatic stress may be carried in the body and expressed through arousal, avoidance, dissociation, memory, emotion, and physical responses. These adaptations are not signs of weakness. They may be the body’s efforts to survive conditions that once felt inescapable.

Gabor Maté’s work invites attention to the inner injury created when people must disconnect from emotions, needs, or authentic parts of themselves in order to preserve attachment or survive adversity. This perspective helps shift the question from “What is wrong with you?” toward “What happened, how did you adapt, and what do you need now?”

Trauma is also shaped by context. Families, schools, workplaces, health systems, justice systems, and communities can either provide safety and repair or reproduce instability, exclusion, and harm. Trauma-responsive practice recognizes individual experience while examining the conditions surrounding it.

No single response defines trauma. People may experience similar events differently, and resilience does not erase harm. A trauma-responsive approach makes room for both vulnerability and strength, prioritizes choice and dignity, and builds pathways toward connection, accountability, support, and healing.

References

Herman, J. L. (1992/2022). Trauma and Recovery. Basic Books.

Maté, G., with Maté, D. (2022). The Myth of Normal. Avery.

van der Kolk, B. A. (2014). The Body Keeps the Score. Viking.
